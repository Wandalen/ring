Ring against rtrb, and the lean-consumer regime

rtrb (the rtrb 0.4 crate) is a published single-producer, single-consumer ring. On the M4 its one-record hand-off at 64 slots ran about 280 M/s. Ring with #41 and #38 (CopySlot, 8-byte slots) ran about 150. This section takes the two rings apart one difference at a time. No difference in the code accounts for the gap. The gap belongs to a regime instead: it exists only while the consumer does no work per record. Why rtrb is fast in that regime is still open.

Measured 2026-10-11/12 on the M4 Pro: rustc 1.97/1.98, fat-LTO bench profile, loop heads pinned with -C llvm-args=-align-loops=16. The placement harness (#51) puts the ring at byte 0 or byte 64 of a 256-byte block, so its two cursors (64-byte padding) share one 128-byte line or sit on two. Two threads, one record per hand-off (push1_pop1), M records/s, higher is better.

Same protocol, different speed

On paper the two rings do the same work per record. Both cache the other end's cursor, on both sides. Both write one slot and do one release store per record. Both reload a cursor only when their cache says full or empty. So the gap has to come from something the protocol leaves open: the slot size, the shape of the consumer, the shape of the producer, the order of the two cursors, the generated code, or where things land in memory. The investigation tested each one on its own.

The probe that flipped the result

A sampling probe was meant to show how full the ring runs. It added about 20 instructions per record to every consumer, and that was enough to change who wins.

Without the probe: the counter run above. With it: the range over the probe's runs. Cycles are per record.

With the probe, rtrb fell from 257 to 100–114 M/s (15 to 37–41 cycles per record) and lost to ring today, which barely moved: 130–152 M/s, 28 to 28–32 cycles. The fill-level samples that probe took come from the slow regime, so none of them is used.

rtrb's lead needs a consumer that does almost nothing per record. Call that the lean-consumer regime. It can be swept.

Table: the sweep
  • rtrb's 64-slot lead exists only at zero consumer work. From one step ring with CopySlot is level or ahead; from four both ring variants beat rtrb.
  • With heavy consumer work the two rings with 8-byte slots fall into the full-ring lockstep (refused pushes 0.1–0.3 per record). TypedSlot's slower producer avoids it.
  • Merely compiling the knob in, set to 0, moved rtrb's baseline from ~280 to 214. This cell is extremely sensitive to generated code.

Why do rtrb's threads pay 4–6x less per miss with a nearly empty consumer? The counters see the same number of misses on both rings and cannot say which line misses. Nothing measured so far answers it.

Tested and dropped: capacity in the producer

Rung F kept the ring's capacity in a field of the producer, and at 64 slots on one line it lifted ring from 149 to 189 M/s. The same change was then tried on the shipped path, with ring's own consumer.

Keeping capacity in ring's producer helps the two-line layout a little (0 to +15%). It costs CopySlot's single-thread push_pop 16% (2.56 → 2.98 ns) and its whole-lap drain 10% (1280 → 1150 M/s), and TypedSlot's one-line 64-slot hand-off about 15%. Dropped, no PR.

The same knob on Zen 3 (#55)

On x86 CI, Bench Compare put #41 at −36…−42% against master on an AMD EPYC 7763 (Zen 3) for the two-thread one-record hand-off, and at −74% with 16384 slots. On the M4 and on a Zen 5 runner #41 gains. Issue #55 asks why. It answers part of it by sweeping the same consumer-work knob on CI.

16384 slots was not swept. Bench Compare's −74% there has no consumer-work reading yet.

The M4 and Zen 3 gaps share a shape. On the M4, rtrb's lead over ring holds only with a lean consumer, and from four steps both ring variants beat it. On Zen 3, #41's loss against master holds only with a lean consumer and crosses zero at about four. Whether one mechanism drives both is not measured.
